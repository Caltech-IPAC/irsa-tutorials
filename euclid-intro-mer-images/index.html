Euclid DR1: MER Mosaics

Learning Goals¶

By the end of this tutorial, you will:

  • understand the basic characteristics of Euclid DR1 MER mosaics;

  • understand the difference between wide and deep MER tiles;

  • know how to identify the official wide survey mosaic when a tile has several mosaics per band;

  • know how to access full MER mosaics from the cloud;

  • know how to make smaller cutouts of MER mosaics;

  • know how to use matplotlib to plot a grid of cutouts;

  • know how to make a color composite from the Euclid VIS and NISP cutouts;

  • know how to identify sources in the cutouts and make basic measurements.

Introduction¶

Euclid launched in July 2023 as a European Space Agency (ESA) mission with involvement by NASA. The primary science goals of Euclid are to better understand the composition and evolution of the dark Universe. The Euclid mission is providing space-based imaging and spectroscopy as well as supporting ground-based imaging to achieve these primary goals. These data are archived by multiple global repositories, including IRSA, where they will support transformational work in many areas of astrophysics.

Euclid Data Release 1 (DR1) comprises about 1900 sq deg of imaging, spectroscopy, and catalogs from the Euclid Wide Survey (EWS), which will eventually cover about 14,000 sq deg, and from the Euclid Deep Survey (EDS), which covers about 53 sq deg in the three Euclid Deep Fields (North, South, and Fornax). DR1 also includes several photometric redshift and instrument calibration fields, such as COSMOS, CDFS, and AEGIS. It follows Quick Release 1 (Q1), which covered about 63 sq deg with a single pass over each of the three Euclid Deep Fields and the dark cloud LDN 1641; all of the DR1 data, including the areas observed in Q1, have been reprocessed with updated pipelines and calibrations.

Among the data products included in DR1 are the Level 2 MER mosaics. MER (the Merge processing function of the Euclid ground segment) combines the VIS, NISP, and ground-based images into mosaics on a common grid, and detects and measures the sources on them to build the MER catalog. The mosaics are created from images taken with the Euclid instruments (VIS and NISP), as well as a variety of ground-based surveys (DES, CFIS, Pan-STARRS, and HSC-based surveys) that provide the u, g, r, i, and z bands. All of the mosaics have been created according to a uniform tiling on the sky, and mapped to a common pixel scale of 0.1 arcsec. The MER processing for DR1 is described in detail in Euclid Collaboration: Kümmel et al. (2026), see the References section below. The release as a whole is described in Euclid Collaboration: Aussel et al. (2026) and the surveys it covers in Euclid Collaboration: Tereno et al. (2026). The VIS, NISP, and ground-based images that go into the mosaics are described in Euclid Collaboration: McCracken et al. (2026), Polenta et al. (2026), and Corcho-Caballero et al. (2026), respectively. This notebook provides a quick introduction to accessing MER mosaics from IRSA. If you have questions about it, please contact the IRSA helpdesk.

Wide and deep MER tiles¶

DR1 MER mosaics come in two kinds of tiles, which IRSA serves as two separate collections:

  • Wide tiles (euclid_dr1_wide_DpdMerBksMosaic): tile IDs start with 102, about 32 x 32 arcmin, the same tiling as in Q1.

  • Deep tiles (euclid_dr1_deep_DpdMerBksMosaic): tile IDs start with 101, about 17 x 17 arcmin, used for fields too deep to process as wide tiles: the Euclid Deep Fields and auxiliary fields such as COSMOS, CDFS, and AEGIS. In the Euclid Deep Fields, they stack all of the observations (about 8 times the VIS exposure time of a wide tile), so they reach much fainter sources.

The deep fields are also covered by wide tiles, and because they have been observed many times, these wide tiles have several mosaics in every band. At most one of them is the official wide survey mosaic; the others are each built from a single pass over the deep field. At our position, only one of the two overlapping wide tiles has its official mosaic in the collection, which the code below selects. The PPLANID keyword in the FITS header tells them apart: the official wide survey mosaic has -DATA_RELEASE- in it, the others DATA_RELEASE_DEEP.

In the MER catalog (see the MER catalogs notebook), the sources of the wide_survey and wide_mode tables are measured on the wide mosaics, and those of the deep_survey and deep_mode tables on the deep mosaics.

Data volume¶

Each wide MER mosaic is approximately 1.47 GB, and each deep MER mosaic is approximately 0.42 GB. Downloading can take some time. Running the full notebook takes about 20 minutes, most of it spent reading data from the cloud.

The two DR1 MER collections are euclid_dr1_wide_DpdMerBksMosaic and euclid_dr1_deep_DpdMerBksMosaic; the others hold the Q1 and Early Release Observations data and other Euclid data products, such as calibrated frames and spectra. We search both DR1 MER collections, one for the deep and one for the wide tiles. For more information, please read the IRSA User Guide.

2. Locate the mosaics in the cloud¶

IRSA hosts the DR1 MER mosaics in an AWS S3 bucket. The cloud_access column of the search results gives the bucket name and file location of each mosaic, so let’s extract the S3 file path from it:

def get_s3_fpath(cloud_access):
    cloud_info = json.loads(cloud_access)  # converts str to dict
    bucket_name = cloud_info['aws']['bucket_name']
    key = cloud_info['aws']['key']

    return f'{bucket_name}/{key}'


deep_science_images['s3_fpath'] = [get_s3_fpath(row['cloud_access']) for row in deep_science_images]
wide_science_images['s3_fpath'] = [get_s3_fpath(row['cloud_access']) for row in wide_science_images]

A few files listed in the search results may not be available in the bucket yet, so we check that each file exists and skip the ones that do not:

s3 = s3fs.S3FileSystem(anon=True)


def drop_missing(images):
    exists = np.array([s3.exists(path) for path in images['s3_fpath']], dtype=bool)
    for obs_id in images['obs_id'][~exists]:
        print(f'Not in the bucket, skipping: {obs_id}')
    return images[exists]


deep_science_images = drop_missing(deep_science_images)
wide_science_images = drop_missing(wide_science_images)

Because our position is in the Euclid Deep Field North, which has been observed many times, each wide tile has several VIS mosaics: the official wide survey mosaic and mosaics built from single passes over the deep field (see Wide and deep MER tiles above). To find the official wide survey VIS mosaic, we read the PPLANID keyword from the header of each wide VIS mosaic returned by our search. 6. Define cutout parameters for a smaller region of interest¶

Each cutout is read directly from the cloud, without downloading the full mosaic, but reading the cutouts in all of the bands can still take a few minutes. Larger cutouts take longer. A cutout cannot be larger than the tile itself (about 17 arcmin for deep tiles and 32 arcmin for wide tiles); near the edge of a tile it is trimmed to the part that overlaps the tile.

######################## User defined section ############################
# Set the image cutout size
im_cutout = 1.0 * u.arcmin

# Set the cutout center coordinates, here our search position
coords_cutout = coord

##########################################################################


def make_cutout(s3_fpath):
    # Read in only the cutout of the image that you want, without downloading the full file
    with fits.open(f's3://{s3_fpath}', fsspec_kwargs={'anon': True}) as hdu:
        header = hdu[0].header
        cutout_data = Cutout2D(hdu[0].section, position=coords_cutout, size=im_cutout, wcs=WCS(header))

    # Define a new fits file based on this smaller cutout, with accurate WCS based on the cutout size
    new_hdu = fits.PrimaryHDU(data=cutout_data.data, header=header)
    new_hdu.header.update(cutout_data.wcs.to_header())
    return new_hdu


# Iterate through each filter
cutout_list = [make_cutout(s3_fpath) for s3_fpath in deep_science_images['s3_fpath']]

Make a color composite¶

Because all MER mosaics share the same pixel grid, we can combine the deep cutouts directly into a false-color RGB image, with NISP H as red, NISP J as green, and VIS as blue.

def scale_band(filt):

    return ImageNormalize(image_data, interval=PercentileInterval(99.5), stretch=AsinhStretch(), clip=True)(image_data)

Compare the deep and wide VIS cutouts¶

Let’s plot the same region in all of the VIS mosaics of the wide tile: first the official wide survey mosaic, then the mosaics built from single passes over the deep field, and finally the deep mosaic. All of them are in the same units, so we display them with the same linear stretch from -2 to +5 times the sky noise of the official wide mosaic. This makes the noise visible: the wide mosaics look grainy, while the deep mosaic is smoother and reveals more faint sources. The sky noise of each mosaic, measured with sigma clipping, is given in the panel titles. The official wide survey mosaic is itself built from a single wide survey visit, so its noise is similar to that of the single-pass mosaics; small differences between them come from the observing conditions of each visit. Artifacts such as satellite trails can appear in any single visit. This cell reads a cutout from each of the wide VIS mosaics of the tile, so it can take several minutes to run.

    e = Ellipse(xy=(sources_thr['x'][i], sources_thr['y'][i]),
                width=6*sources_thr['a'][i],
                height=6*sources_thr['b'][i],
                angle=sources_thr['theta'][i] * 180. / np.pi)
    e.set_facecolor('none')
    e.set_edgecolor('red')
    ax.add_artist(e)
