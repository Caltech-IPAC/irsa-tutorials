Euclid DR1: MER Catalogs

Learning Goals¶

By the end of this tutorial, you will:

  • Understand the basic characteristics of Euclid DR1 MER catalogs.

  • Know which of the four DR1 MER catalog tables to use.

  • What columns are available in the MER catalog.

  • How to search the MER catalog around a position on the sky.

  • How to use the MER quality columns to clean a catalog.

  • How to select stars and compute their total fluxes in the Euclid and ground-based bands.

  • How to look at the MER VIS images of catalog sources.

  • How to query the MER catalog with ADQL and make a color-color diagram of stars.

Introduction¶

Euclid launched in July 2023 as a European Space Agency (ESA) mission with involvement by NASA. The primary science goals of Euclid are to better understand the composition and evolution of the dark Universe. The Euclid mission is providing space-based imaging and spectroscopy as well as supporting ground-based imaging to achieve these primary goals. These data are archived by multiple global repositories, including IRSA, where they will support transformational work in many areas of astrophysics.

Euclid Data Release 1 (DR1) comprises about 1900 sq deg of imaging, spectroscopy, and catalogs from the Euclid Wide Survey (EWS), which will eventually cover about 14,000 sq deg, and from the Euclid Deep Survey (EDS), which covers about 53 sq deg in the three Euclid Deep Fields (North, South, and Fornax). DR1 also includes several photometric redshift and instrument calibration fields, such as COSMOS, CDFS, and AEGIS. It follows Quick Release 1 (Q1), which covered about 63 sq deg with a single pass over each of the three Euclid Deep Fields and the dark cloud LDN 1641; all of the DR1 data, including the areas observed in Q1, have been reprocessed with updated pipelines and calibrations.

Among the data products included in DR1 are the MER catalogs. MER (the Merge processing function of the Euclid ground segment) combines the VIS, NISP, and ground-based images into mosaics on a common grid, and detects and measures the sources on them to build the MER catalog. The MER processing detects sources on the VIS and NISP mosaics (see the MER mosaics notebook) and measures their photometry in the Euclid VIS, Y, J, H bands and in the accompanying ground-based u, g, r, i, z bands, along with other basic measurements, like size and shape. This notebook provides an introduction to the MER final catalog, in which each entry is a single source. In this notebook we follow a typical path through the MER catalog: we search for the sources around a position on the sky, clean the sample with the quality columns, select the stars, look at the spectral energy distributions and VIS images of a few of them, and finally make a color-color diagram of many stars. The MER processing for DR1 is described in detail in Euclid Collaboration: Kümmel et al. (2026), see the References section below. If you have questions about this notebook, please contact the IRSA helpdesk.

The MER catalog tables¶

The DR1 MER catalog is split into four tables, according to how the data were observed and which MER tiling was used, plus two companion photometry tables for the deep tiles. MER processes the sky in tiles: wide tiles (tile IDs starting with 102, about 32 x 32 arcmin, the same tiling as in Q1) and smaller deep tiles (tile IDs starting with 101, about 17 x 17 arcmin), used for fields too deep to process as wide tiles.

  • euclid_dr1_mer_catalogue_wide_survey -- the Euclid Wide Survey, the standard-depth survey intended for cosmology (wide tiles). If you are looking for the standard Euclid survey, this is the table you want, and it is the one used in this notebook.

  • euclid_dr1_mer_catalogue_deep_survey -- the Euclid Deep Survey: the deep stacks of the Euclid Deep Fields North, South, and Fornax (deep tiles).

  • euclid_dr1_mer_catalogue_deep_mode -- other fields deep enough to need the deep tiling, but not part of the Deep Survey: the Euclid auxiliary fields COSMOS, CDFS, and AEGIS.

  • euclid_dr1_mer_catalogue_wide_mode -- other fields processed with the wide tiling that are neither part of the Wide Survey nor visits of the deep fields, for example some of the fields observed during the thermal stabilization period of the PSF calibration campaign, just before the main survey started (January/February 2024).

  • euclid_dr1_mer_catalogue_deep_survey_photometry and euclid_dr1_mer_catalogue_deep_mode_photometry -- companion tables for the deep tiles, with photometry in the additional external bands that are only available in the deep and auxiliary fields (for example Spitzer/IRAC, GALEX, VISTA, and the Subaru intermediate bands). They can be joined to the corresponding main table on object_id.

The wide_survey and wide_mode sources are measured on the wide-tile mosaics, and the deep_survey and deep_mode sources on the deep-tile mosaics. In the deep fields, the wide tiles also have single-pass “deep visit” mosaics (see the MER mosaics notebook); the wide-tile catalog in these fields comes from the official wide survey mosaic only.

The deep fields are covered by both the Deep Survey (deep tiles) and the wide tiling, so the same object can appear in more than one table, measured on data of different depth. Table 5 of Kümmel et al. (2026) lists all of the DR1 fields with their tiling and exposure times.

The MER catalog from the earlier Quick Release 1 (Q1) is also available at IRSA, as the single table euclid_q1_mer_catalogue. All of the Q1 areas have been reprocessed for DR1, so Q1 and DR1 fluxes should not be mixed (see the notes on the DR1 MER photometry below).

2. Search the MER catalog around a position¶

We start with all of the sources within 0.5 deg of a position in the Wide Survey. This area contains a few hundred thousand sources, so rather than retrieving all 480 columns, we write an ADQL query that returns only the columns we need for this notebook: the positions and tile IDs, the quality columns, the star-galaxy classification, and the template-fitting fluxes in the Euclid and ground-based bands (see section 5). The CONTAINS and CIRCLE functions select the sources in a circle on the sky.

coord = SkyCoord(ra=90.7299, dec=-45.3529, unit='deg')
search_radius = 0.5 * u.deg

# Template-fitting fluxes in the Euclid NIR bands and in all of the ground-based bands
flux_cols = ([f'flux_{band}_templfit' for band in 'yjh'] + [f'flux_vis_to_{band}_templfit' for band in 'yjh']
             + [col for col in columns_info
                if col.startswith('flux_') and '_ext_' in col and col.endswith('_templfit')])
columns = (['object_id', 'ra', 'dec', 'tileid', 'vis_det', 'flux_detection_total',
            'point_like_flag', 'point_like_prob', 'spurious_prob', 'det_quality_flag',

3. Clean the sample with the quality columns¶

Before using the sources, we check the MER columns that identify sources whose detection or photometry may be unreliable:

  • spurious_prob is the probability that a source is a spurious detection, for example an artifact around a bright object. spurious_flag is set for spurious_prob > 0.5, but this has proven too aggressive, and a threshold of 0.7 is suggested instead (see the notes on the DR1 MER photometry below).

  • flag_vis, flag_y, flag_j, and flag_h keep track of the flagged pixels (for example bad or saturated pixels) in the flag image of each band.

  • det_quality_flag flags problems in the detection step that could corrupt the magnitude used for the star-galaxy separation.

Nonzero flags do not necessarily mean that a source is unusable, but they are worth checking for your science case.

is_spurious = result['spurious_prob'] > 0.7
print(f"spurious_prob > 0.5: {np.sum(result['spurious_prob'] > 0.5)} of {len(result)} sources")
print(f"spurious_prob > 0.7: {np.sum(is_spurious)} of {len(result)} sources")
print(f"flag_vis != 0: {np.sum(result['flag_vis'] != 0)} of {len(result)} sources")
print(f"det_quality_flag != 0: {np.sum(result['det_quality_flag'] != 0)} of {len(result)} sources")

4. Select the stars¶

The MER catalog does not have a column for classification, but it provides point_like_prob, the probability that a VIS-detected source is point-like. point_like_flag = 1 selects VIS-detected sources with point_like_prob > 0.96, the threshold that reached a 99% star purity in simulations. A lower point_like_prob threshold gives a more complete but less pure star sample.

# point_like_flag is only set for VIS-detected sources; masked values count as 0
stars = clean_sources[(np.ma.filled(clean_sources['point_like_flag'], 0) == 1)
                      & (clean_sources['vis_det'] == 1)]
print(len(stars))
2964

5. Look at the SEDs and VIS images of three stars¶

Total fluxes in the Euclid and ground-based bands¶

Besides the Euclid VIS, Y, J, and H bands, the MER catalog contains photometry measured on ground-based images in the u, g, r, i, and z bands. Which ground-based surveys are available depends on the position on the sky, for example DECam in the south, or a combination of MegaCam, HSC, and Pan-STARRS in the north. The catalog therefore has columns for all of the possible survey and band combinations, named flux_<band>_ext_<survey>_..., and only some of them are filled at a given position. Let’s check which ground-based bands have measurements for our stars.

At this position, the ground-based photometry comes from DECam, in the g, r, i, and z bands.

To get total fluxes, we follow the recipe of the MER Photometry Cookbook in the Euclid DR1 Data Product Description Document (DPDD). The total flux of an object is measured with a Kron aperture in the detection band, which is VIS for VIS-detected objects (vis_det = 1), and is given in flux_detection_total. The total flux in another band X, from Euclid or from the ground, is then obtained by scaling the detection-band total flux with the color measured by template fitting:

FX,tot=flux_detection_total×flux_X_templfitflux_vis_to_X_templfitF_{X,\mathrm{tot}} = \mathtt{flux\_detection\_total} \times \frac{\mathtt{flux\_X\_templfit}}{\mathtt{flux\_vis\_to\_X\_templfit}}FX,tot​=flux_detection_total×flux_vis_to_X_templfitflux_X_templfit​
(1)

where flux_vis_to_X_templfit is the template-fitting flux measured on the VIS image degraded to the resolution of band X. The cookbook gives an equivalent recipe with the aperture fluxes (flux_X_nfwhm_aper / flux_vis_nfwhm_aper). The fluxes are in uJy, which we convert to AB magnitudes.

def total_mag(sources, band):
    """Total AB magnitude of VIS-detected sources in a band, following the MER Photometry Cookbook."""
    flux = sources['flux_detection_total']
    if band != 'vis':
        flux = flux * sources[f'flux_{band}_templfit'] / sources[f'flux_vis_to_{band}_templfit']
    return -2.5 * np.log10(flux) + 23.9


def has_positive_fluxes(sources, bands):
    """True for sources whose fluxes used by total_mag are all positive (masked fluxes count as missing)."""
    cols = ['flux_detection_total']
    for band in bands:
        if band != 'vis':
            cols += [f'flux_{band}_templfit', f'flux_vis_to_{band}_templfit']
    return np.all([np.ma.filled(sources[col], 0) > 0 for col in cols], axis=0)

Choose three stars¶

We pick three stars with positive fluxes in all bands, no flagged pixels in the Euclid bands, no detection problems, and of intermediate brightness. To span the range of stellar colors, we take the stars closest to three points along the stellar locus in the VIS - J vs. J - H color-color diagram that we make below: a blue, an intermediate, and a red star.

# Approximate central wavelengths of the bands, in micron
band_wavelengths = {'g_ext_decam': 0.48, 'r_ext_decam': 0.64, 'vis': 0.72, 'i_ext_decam': 0.78,
                    'z_ext_decam': 0.92, 'y': 1.08, 'j': 1.37, 'h': 1.77}

has_all_bands = has_positive_fluxes(stars, band_wavelengths)
mag_vis = total_mag(stars, 'vis')
no_flags = ((stars['flag_vis'] == 0) & (stars['flag_y'] == 0) & (stars['flag_j'] == 0) & (stars['flag_h'] == 0)
            & (stars['det_quality_flag'] == 0))
candidates = stars[has_all_bands & no_flags & (mag_vis > 18.5) & (mag_vis < 21)]

color_vis_j = total_mag(candidates, 'vis') - total_mag(candidates, 'j')
color_j_h = total_mag(candidates, 'j') - total_mag(candidates, 'h')
# (VIS - J, J - H) of a blue, an intermediate, and a red star on the stellar locus
locus_points = [(0.0, -0.2), (0.5, -0.03), (1.5, 0.03)]
three_stars = candidates[[np.argmin(np.hypot(color_vis_j - x, color_j_h - y)) for x, y in locus_points]]

The MER object_id is built from the position of the source: its digits are the RA and the absolute value of the Dec, both in units of 1e-7 deg, and its sign is the sign of the Dec. All of our sources are in the southern sky, so they have negative IDs.

Get VIS cutouts of the three stars¶

The MER mosaics are available through IRSA’s Simple Image Access (SIA) service and are hosted in the cloud (see the MER mosaics notebook for more details). For each star, we search for the VIS mosaics at its position (in the wide or deep collection, depending on its tile), and read a small cutout directly from the cloud, without downloading the full mosaic. Each source in the MER catalog belongs to one tile, so we use the mosaic of the tile given by its tileid.

def get_s3_fpath(cloud_access):
    cloud_info = json.loads(cloud_access)  # converts str to dict
    return f"{cloud_info['aws']['bucket_name']}/{cloud_info['aws']['key']}"


def vis_cutout(source, size=10 * u.arcsec):
    position = SkyCoord(ra=source['ra'], dec=source['dec'], unit='deg')
    # Deep tile IDs start with 101, wide tile IDs with 102
    if str(source['tileid']).startswith('101'):
        collection = 'euclid_dr1_deep_DpdMerBksMosaic'
    else:
        collection = 'euclid_dr1_wide_DpdMerBksMosaic'
    sia_results = Irsa.query_sia(pos=(position, 1 * u.arcsec), collection=collection)
    # The tile ID is the first part of the obs_id of the mosaic
    is_vis_mosaic = ((sia_results['dataproduct_subtype'] == 'science') & (sia_results['energy_bandpassname'] == 'VIS')
                     & [obs_id.startswith(str(source['tileid'])) for obs_id in sia_results['obs_id']])
    mosaic = sia_results[is_vis_mosaic][0]
    with fits.open(f"s3://{get_s3_fpath(mosaic['cloud_access'])}", fsspec_kwargs={'anon': True}) as hdu:
        return Cutout2D(hdu[0].section, position=position, size=size, wcs=WCS(hdu[0].header)).data


cutouts = [vis_cutout(star) for star in three_stars]

The stars follow the stellar locus, a narrow sequence in color-color space set mainly by their temperature. From hot to cool stars, both colors first become redder together, then J - H stays nearly constant while VIS - J keeps increasing: for the coolest stars, the M dwarfs, molecular absorption shapes the near-infrared spectrum and J - H no longer tracks temperature. The longer wavelength baseline of VIS - J keeps separating these stars, and our three stars lie at different positions along the locus. The ground-based g - r color, which also traces temperature, changes smoothly along the locus, from the blue, hot stars to the red, cool ones.

Notes on the DR1 MER photometry¶

Kümmel et al. (2026) describe the DR1 MER photometry and its known limitations in detail. The most important points for using the catalog are:

  • NIR calibration offsets: the Euclid Y, J, and H fluxes have offsets of a few to about 13 percent with respect to Gaia, depending on the photometric method. The offsets are similar in the three bands, so NIR colors are more reliable than absolute NIR magnitudes.

  • Partial coverage: some tiles are only partially covered, at the survey borders or in individual bands, for example after solar proton events. Requiring a detection in all bands therefore removes sources in these areas.

  • Spurious sources: spurious_flag is set for spurious_prob > 0.5, which has proven too aggressive; a threshold of 0.7 is suggested instead. The spurious probability is optimized for wide data.

Note

If you have used the Euclid Quick Release 1 (Q1) MER catalog: the DR1 PSFs are no longer normalized to unit flux. This corrects the large offsets seen in Q1 for bands with extended PSFs (such as Pan-STARRS i), but means that DR1 fluxes differ systematically from the Q1 ones, so the two releases should not be mixed. Colors measured with aperture (A-PHOT) or template-fitting (T-PHOT) photometry are largely unaffected.
